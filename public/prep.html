Level 1: the foundations

Master this tab before moving on. If you can explain the worked example out loud, you're ready for Intermediate.

What is DeFi, and what is a DEX?

DeFi = financial services (trading, lending, earning yield) rebuilt as smart contracts on a blockchain. No company holds your money; code holds it, and anyone can use or build on it.

CEX (Binance, Coinbase): a company has custody and runs an order book matching buyers with sellers. DEX (Raydium, Uniswap): non-custodial smart contracts, and most don't match buyers and sellers at all; they use an AMM.

The AMM: the one concept that matters most

No order book. Instead, liquidity pools: smart contracts holding two tokens. A formula prices every trade: x × y = k. Spot price = y / x.

So a pool holding 1,000 SOL and 76,000 USDC prices SOL at $76. Buy SOL from the pool and SOL gets scarcer, so the price rises automatically. You trade against the pot, not a person.

The worked example (be able to do this out loud)

Pool: 1,000 SOL and 76,000 USDC, so k = 76,000,000. You sell 10 SOL (ignore fees).

  • New SOL: 1,010. USDC must become 76,000,000 / 1,010 = 75,247.5.
  • You receive 76,000 − 75,247.5 = 752.5 USDC, i.e. $75.25/SOL instead of $76.
  • That gap is price impact: your own trade moved the price.

Rule of thumb from Raydium's docs: impact ≈ 2 × (trade size / pool size). Trade 1% of the pool, move price ~2%.

Liquidity providers: who fills the pot, and why

LPs deposit both tokens into a pool and earn a cut of every trade's fee (on Raydium's standard pools, most of a 0.25% fee per trade). Deep pools = better prices for traders = more volume = more fees. That flywheel is the whole business.

The catch is a risk called impermanent loss. That's the first thing in the Intermediate tab.

Slippage tolerance: the safety net on every swap

Prices move between the moment you're quoted and the moment your trade lands. Slippage tolerance (e.g. 0.5%) sets the worst fill you'll accept: the transaction carries a minimum-out amount and fails on purpose rather than filling worse. In my quickstart, that's the otherAmountThreshold field.

Level 2: the trade-offs

This is where LP economics and routing live: the topics an interviewer uses to separate "read a blog once" from "gets it".

Impermanent loss, with numbers

The AMM formula constantly rebalances the pool, which means LPs automatically sell whichever token is rising and accumulate the one that's falling. If prices diverge, an LP ends up worth less than someone who just held both tokens.

The numbers to quote: if one token's price doubles, the LP is worth about 5.7% less than holding. 4x price move: about 20% less. Price returns to entry: loss disappears, hence "impermanent".

The judgment line: LPing is profitable when fee income beats divergence. Stable pairs: tiny IL, tiny fees. Volatile pairs: big fees needed to compensate.

Slippage vs price impact (interviewers love this distinction)
  • Price impact: what your own trade does to the pool. Deterministic, comes from the formula, exists even if you're the only trader on earth.
  • Slippage: the world changing between your quote and your execution because other trades landed first.
  • Tight tolerance also happens to be the cheapest defense against sandwich attacks (Pro tab).
Concentrated liquidity (CLMM): the concept

Standard AMM liquidity is spread across every price from 0 to infinity, so most of it sits where trading never happens. A CLMM lets each LP concentrate into a chosen price range, e.g. $70–$85 for SOL/USDC.

  • Win: far deeper liquidity per dollar inside the range. I watched a $6M CLMM pool out-trade a $10M standard pool on Raydium, live.
  • Catch: price exits your range and you earn nothing while holding 100% of the losing side. Concentration concentrates IL too. It's active management, not passive.

The internals (ticks, sqrt prices, fee growth) are in the Pro tab.

Routing: multi-hop and aggregators
  • Multi-hop: no good direct pool? Route A → B → C through a hub token (USDC, SOL, USDT). Each hop adds its own fee and impact.
  • Your live example: quoting 0.1 SOL → USDC, Raydium's Trade API routed SOL → USDT → USDC in two hops because it beat the direct pool.
  • Aggregators: Jupiter shops every Solana DEX for the best route; ~60% of its routed volume flows through Raydium. That's why integrators matter so much to them.
Fee tiers: why different pools charge differently

Fees compensate LPs for risk. Stable pairs (USDC/USDT) barely move, so competition drives fees to 0.01%. Volatile meme pairs need 1%+ to pay for the IL risk. Mid-volatility majors sit around 0.25%. On CLMMs the fee tier also pairs with tick spacing: tighter spacing for stables, wider for volatile pairs.

Level 3: under the hood

Everything here is from Raydium's own protocol docs. One or two of these dropped naturally in the interview signals real depth. Don't force them all in.

CLMM internals: ticks and sqrt prices
  • Prices sit on a grid of ticks: price(i) = 1.0001i, with indices from about −443,636 to +443,636. Positions are ranges between ticks, aligned to the pool's tick spacing (1 for the 0.01% tier, 60 for 0.25%, 120 for 1%).
  • Raydium stores the square root of price in Q64.64 fixed point, not the price itself. Why: token amounts become linear functions of sqrt-price (cheap math per swap), products like sqrt_price × liquidity don't overflow, and tick steps become an exact power ladder.
  • Within one tick the pool behaves like a tiny constant-product AMM. When a swap crosses an initialized tick, the pool adds or subtracts that tick's net liquidity and keeps going. That's why CLMM swap compute cost varies so much (170–375k CU).
CLMM internals: how LPs get paid

The pool keeps a global fee growth counter per token (fees per unit of liquidity, accumulated forever). Each tick tracks fee growth "outside" itself, flipping when crossed. A position computes fees earned inside its range as global minus outside-below minus outside-above, times its liquidity. Elegant: no per-position bookkeeping on every swap.

Raydium extras beyond the Uniswap v3 design: Token-2022 support, tick bitmaps packed for fast lookup, and up to three independent reward streams per pool with the same growth-counter pattern.

Constant product internals: fees, reserves, rounding
  • Fee comes off your input first, then the remainder trades against the curve: output = y × Δx_after_fee / (x + Δx_after_fee).
  • LP fees stay in the reserves (that's how LP value grows); protocol/fund shares accrue separately and are excluded from pricing. Integration gotcha from their docs: when quoting AMM v4 from vault balances, subtract the accrued protocol fees (need_take_pnl) or your price is slightly wrong.
  • Rounding rules: fees round up, outputs round down, exact-output inputs round up. The pool never undercharges. Off-by-one bug reports from integrators are usually this.
Routing internals: how the router actually decides
  • Split routing divides a big trade across pools so the marginal price equalizes across them. Their docs: under 0.1% of pool TVL, single pool is fine; at 1–5% of TVL, splitting wins 10–50 basis points. A greedy algorithm over 100–500 slices gets near-optimal.
  • Multi-hop impacts compound multiplicatively, not additively, and each hop pays its own fee. Deep hops through hubs still often beat shallow direct pools.
MEV mechanics: sandwiches and defenses, properly
  • Solana has no public ordered mempool. Front-running happens via leader-side reordering and co-located searchers watching transaction packets arrive.
  • Sandwich: bot buys before you, you fill worse, bot exits. Only profitable when your trade moves price meaningfully and your slippage tolerance leaves room; bots hunt for 1–2%+ tolerance.
  • Defenses, in order: tight slippage (≤0.3% reverts most sandwiches), Jito bundles (private submission, never hits gossip, validator tip ~0.0001–0.001 SOL), fresh pool state right before submit, split anything over ~1% of a single pool's TVL.
  • Neat detail: deep CLMM liquidity naturally resists sandwiches, because trades that stay within a tick barely move price.
Token-2022 transfer fees (a current integrator pain)

Token-2022 mints can take a fee on every transfer, so the amount a pool receives is less than the amount you sent. Swap math has to price on post-transfer-fee amounts or quotes drift from reality. It also costs 25–30% more compute than classic SPL. CPMM is Raydium's Token-2022-native pool, which is part of why it's the recommended standard pool now.

The mental model

Accounts, programs, instructions, transactions
  • Everything is an account. Data lives in accounts. Programs are accounts holding code. Your wallet is an account holding SOL.
  • Programs are stateless. They only read and write the accounts a transaction hands them. That's why Solana txs list every account they touch, marked writable or read-only, signer or not. It's also what lets Solana run transactions in parallel: two txs touching different accounts don't block each other.
  • A transaction = one or more instructions (each: program + accounts + data) + the account list + signatures. All instructions succeed or the whole tx fails atomically.
  • Rent: accounts must hold a minimum SOL balance ("rent-exempt") proportional to their data size. Creating an account costs a small deposit, reclaimable when closed.
Tokens: SPL, ATAs, wrapped SOL, decimals
  • Tokens follow the SPL Token standard (or the newer Token-2022 with extensions like transfer fees).
  • Your "USDC balance" isn't in your wallet account; it's in a separate token account. The canonical one per wallet-per-mint is the ATA (associated token account), derived deterministically. Receiving a token you've never held requires creating the ATA first (~20k CU + rent deposit).
  • Wrapped SOL: native SOL isn't an SPL token, so pools trade WSOL. Integrations wrap before and unwrap after (the Trade API's wrapSol/unwrapSol flags).
  • Decimals matter: amounts are raw integers. SOL has 9 decimals (1 SOL = 1e9 lamports), USDC has 6. In my quickstart, 0.1 SOL went in as 100000000 and 7.62 USDC came back as 7617860. Getting decimals wrong is a classic integrator bug: quotes off by a factor of 1,000.
Transaction lifecycle: build, sign, send, confirm
  • Build: assemble instructions, set a compute budget and priority fee, reference a recent blockhash (the tx's freshness stamp, valid ~150 blocks, roughly 60–90 seconds).
  • Sign: every required signer signs the message. Slow human signing (hardware wallets, multisig) is a common reason the blockhash expires before sending.
  • Send: submit to an RPC node, which forwards toward the current leader. There's no public mempool; unconfirmed txs aren't globally visible.
  • Confirm: poll signature status at a commitment level: processed (one node saw it), confirmed (cluster majority voted, the usual choice), finalized (rooted, ~30+ blocks, irreversible). Mixing commitment levels between writes and reads causes "I sent it but can't see it" confusion.
Versioned transactions and address lookup tables
  • A Solana tx maxes out at 1,232 bytes. Every account address costs 32 bytes, and AMM swaps touch a lot of accounts (pool state, vaults, ATAs, programs, oracles).
  • V0 (versioned) transactions solve this with address lookup tables (ALTs): on-chain address lists a tx can reference by index instead of carrying full addresses.
  • That's exactly what I saw building a Raydium swap: a V0 transaction with 5 instructions, 24 static accounts, and 1 lookup table. Legacy tools that only understand legacy txs choke on these; integrators still hit this.

Making transactions land

Compute budget: the CU system
  • Every instruction consumes compute units. Default budget is 200k CU per instruction unless you set your own limit; the per-transaction max is 1.4M.
  • You set it explicitly with a SetComputeUnitLimit instruction. Blow the limit and you get ProgramFailedToComplete.
  • Raydium's own benchmarks: CPMM swap ~140–150k CU; CLMM swap 170–375k depending on tick crossings; Token-2022 costs 25–30% more; add ~20k per ATA creation. Budget ~25% headroom above expected.
  • Why CLMM varies: each initialized tick the swap crosses is extra account loading and math. A volatile pool at a busy price level costs more CU than a quiet one.
Priority fees: how inclusion actually works
  • Priority fee = micro-lamports per CU, set via SetComputeUnitPrice. It's a bid for the leader's attention on top of the base 5,000 lamports/signature.
  • Fee markets are per writable account, not global. Writing to a hot Raydium pool competes with everyone else writing that pool; a quiet account is nearly free. Estimate against the accounts you touch (getRecentPrioritizationFees or an RPC provider's estimator), not a global average.
  • Retry pattern from Raydium's docs: escalate the fee ~1.5× per attempt, refresh the blockhash, and broadcast to multiple RPCs. Cost reality: a bot doing 1,000 tx/day pays roughly $1–30/month in priority fees, 5–10× that during congestion.
RPC: your only window into the chain
  • An RPC node is how apps read state and submit txs. Methods you'll actually use: getLatestBlockhash, sendTransaction, simulateTransaction, getSignatureStatuses, getAccountInfo, getRecentPrioritizationFees.
  • Public RPCs are rate-limited and lag; production integrators use paid providers (Helius, Triton, QuickNode). "Works on my machine, fails in prod" is often just two different RPCs at different freshness.
  • A node that's behind causes: "blockhash not found" on fresh blockhashes, stale pool state (quotes off), and txs that seem to vanish. Compare against a second RPC before blaming the protocol.
  • simulateTransaction is the debugger's best friend: runs the tx against current state and returns the full program logs, without spending anything.

Debugging like the job demands

Reading a failed transaction (anatomy of the logs)
  • Look the signature up on an explorer (Solscan, Solana Explorer, SolanaFM) or pull it via RPC. The program logs show each program invocation nested, and name exactly which instruction index failed.
  • Failures read like: InstructionError: [2, Custom(6021)] = instruction #2 failed with the program's own error code 6021. Custom codes map to the program's error enum: look them up in the program's IDL or source. Slippage-exceeded errors from AMMs show up this way constantly.
  • Chain-level errors (blockhash expired, insufficient fee-payer SOL, missing signature) never reach a program; they're rejected earlier, which itself tells you where to look.
  • If it never landed at all, there are no logs: that's a delivery problem (priority fee, expired blockhash, RPC), not a program problem. This one distinction sorts half of all support tickets.

The 5-step method (recite this in the interview)
  • 1. Get the artifact. Landed: the tx signature, read the logs. Never landed: the serialized tx + the RPC error. No artifact, no debugging; "it doesn't work" is not an artifact.
  • 2. Reproduce minimally. Strip to the smallest failing call, ideally a standalone script. (My quickstart scripts are exactly this shape, which is why reference repos matter.)
  • 3. Read logs, not vibes. simulateTransaction gives full logs for free. The failing instruction index plus the custom error code usually is the answer.
  • 4. Classify. Delivery problem (fees, blockhash, RPC) vs program problem (slippage, CU, accounts) vs integration problem (request shape, decimals, missing ATA). Each class has a different owner and fix.
  • 5. Fix and document. If one integrator hit it, ten more will. The ticket isn't closed until the answer is in the docs or an example. That last step is the DevRel job.
Support scenarios to rehearse (they may roleplay these)
  • "Swaps work for SOL/USDC but fail for this new token." Think: missing ATA for the output mint, Token-2022 transfer fees breaking the math, or a thin pool where any real size blows the slippage tolerance.
  • "It works in dev, fails in production." Think: different RPC (freshness, rate limits), different commitment level, prod wallet short on SOL, prod traffic hitting contested accounts without priority fees.
  • "Transactions fail only during volume spikes." Think: static priority fees that don't escalate, CU limits sized on quiet CLMM pools that cross more ticks when volatile, blockhashes expiring in retry queues.
  • "Your API is broken, the tx builder rejects my request." Think: request shape (the swapResponse envelope trap I personally hit), stale quote being replayed, wrong txVersion.
  • In every case the script is the same: artifact, minimal repro, logs, classify, fix, document.
